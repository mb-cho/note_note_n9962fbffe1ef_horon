The AIG (American International Group) Crisis:
Why Did a Giant Insurance Company Become the Ground Zero of the Lehman Shock and Get Rescued with Public Funds?

In this context, AIG (American International Group) was an American mega-insurance and financial conglomerate that boasted the world's largest asset base and the highest credit rating at the time.
While its core businesses were traditional life, property, and casualty insurance operating globally, during the 2008 global financial crisis (the Lehman Shock), reckless derivative trading by a financial subsidiary comprising merely several dozen staff members triggered an existential catastrophe that nearly took down the American and global financial systems.

1. Why Did an "Insurance Company" Become the Ground Zero of the Financial Crisis?

The root cause of AIG's collapse was a specialized London-based subsidiary known as AIG Financial Products (AIGFP).

① Monetizing the Crown Jewel: "Top-Tier Credit Rating (AAA)"

At the time, the parent entity of AIG maintained a pristine AAA credit rating, the highest tier of creditworthiness in the global market. Using this parent rating as a corporate storefront, AIGFP sold astronomical volumes of Credit Default Swaps (CDS) to premier Wall Street and European investment banks (including Goldman Sachs, Merrill Lynch, Deutsche Bank, Société Générale, and others).

② Pseudo-Insurance Business via Credit Default Swaps (CDS)

A Credit Default Swap (CDS) is a financial derivative whereby the seller agrees to compensate the buyer if underlying debt instruments—such as mortgage-backed securities (MBS) or collateralized debt obligations (CDO)—experience default or severe impairment.

③ The Delusion of "Free Money" and Unchecked Moral Hazard

AIGFP operated under mathematical risk models asserting that a nationwide simultaneous decline in US housing prices was statistically impossible. Consequently, they held virtually no cash collateral or capital reserves to cover potential payout obligations, treating guarantee premiums as pure, risk-free profits and distributing massive bonus pools to their traders.

2. The Unraveling Mechanism: 4-Stage Spiral to Insolvency

When the US subprime mortgage bubble burst in 2007, the entire derivative superstructure rapidly unraveled in four escalating stages:

STEP 1: Collapse of Underlying Assets
Plummeting CDO Valuations

As delinquencies on subprime mortgages surged, the market value of collateralized debt obligations (CDOs) insured by AIG collapsed toward zero.

STEP 2: Loss of Creditworthiness
Credit Rating Downgrades of AIG

Mounting mark-to-market losses forced credit rating agencies to strip AIG of its vital AAA credit rating.

STEP 3: Avalanche of Margin Calls
Demands for Additional Collateral

Under the terms of the CDS contracts, downgrading triggered contractual covenants requiring AIG to immediately post tens of billions of dollars in cash collateral to its banking counterparties.

STEP 4: Liquidity Deadlock
Cash Depletion (Liquidity Panic)

Faced with simultaneous cash collateral calls totaling tens of billions of dollars from Wall Street and Europe, AIG's liquidity evaporated completely within days.

[Phase 3: September 15–16, 2008 — Rating Downgrades Following Lehman's Bankruptcy and the Liquidity Panic]
Immediately following Lehman Brothers' filing for bankruptcy on September 15, 2008, all three major rating agencies downgraded AIG's credit ratings. AIGFP's CDS contracts contained credit rating triggers: as long as AIG maintained a AAA rating, it was exempt from posting cash collateral; however, the instant its rating was downgraded, it was contractually obligated to post tens of billions of dollars in cash collateral immediately. Demands for immediate cash from counterparties like Goldman Sachs flooded in simultaneously, pushing AIG to the brink of disorderly collapse overnight.

3. "Too Big to Fail" and Wall Street's Backdoor Bailout

On September 16, 2008—the very day after allowing Lehman Brothers to fail—AIG stood completely insolvent and facing imminent bankruptcy.

🏛️ De Facto Nationalization with Over $180 Billion in Public Funds

Having let Lehman fail, the Federal Reserve (FRB) and the US Treasury reversed course completely for AIG, ultimately injecting over $180 billion in taxpayer funds—the largest financial rescue in history—taking a nearly 80% equity stake in a de facto nationalization.

The decision was driven by the realization that because AIG's CDS commitments formed a web spanning the entire global financial architecture, its collapse would inflict catastrophic losses across major US and European banks, causing a complete systemic meltdown of the Western banking system.

💸 100% Full-Par Backdoor Bailout to Goldman Sachs and Counterparties

Crucially, the public taxpayer funds injected into AIG were channeled directly to AIG's trading counterparties—most notably Goldman Sachs, Société Générale, Deutsche Bank, and Merrill Lynch—at 100 cents on the dollar, without requiring a single dollar of haircut (loss-sharing).

Consequently, the intervention was widely condemned by the public and independent observers as a "backdoor bailout," using AIG merely as a conduit to rescue reckless Wall Street mega-banks at public expense while privatizing their previous profits.
