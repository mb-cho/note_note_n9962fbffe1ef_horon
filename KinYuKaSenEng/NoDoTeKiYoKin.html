Actively Created Deposits

In John Maynard Keynes's seminal work A Treatise on Money (1930), the concept of "Actively Created Deposits" was introduced to elucidate the core mechanism of commercial bank credit creation (deposit creation).[1][2][3][4]

Keynes established that deposits held within the banking system possess two fundamentally different origins: "Actively Created Deposits" and "Passively Created Deposits."[1][4]

💡 1. What Are Actively Created Deposits?

These are deposits created directly by banks through their own voluntary initiative ("actively") when extending loans or acquiring securities.[1][4]

🖋️ Mechanism: Creation via Keystrokes
When a bank extends a loan to an enterprise, it does not transport physical cash out of its vault. Rather, by simply writing or typing numbers into the borrower's account, new deposit currency is created out of nothing.[5]
🚀 Economic Implication: "Loans Create Deposits"
This encapsulates the fundamental truth of endogenous money supply (credit creation): "loans create deposits." Far from being passive intermediaries in the business cycle, commercial banks serve as the primary active drivers of monetary expansion.[4][5][6]
KEY PRINCIPLE OF CREDIT CREATION
"Banks do not lend out deposits collected from others. The very act of bank lending creates an equal amount of new deposit currency out of nothing."
― Endogenous Money Theory

🏛️ 3. Why Is This Concept So Crucial?

In contrast to conventional economics' "relending theory" (which asserts that banks merely intermediate by lending out pre-existing customer deposits), Keynes in A Treatise on Money illuminated the actual mechanism of modern credit: commercial banks as a whole determine and expand the total volume of money (deposits) through their own active decisions to lend and invest.[1][4][5]

📈 Establishing Endogenous Money Supply
This conceptualization decisively influenced modern macroeconomics and Post-Keynesian economics, which established Endogenous Money Supply Theory (the principle that money is not an exogenous quantity fixed by the central bank, but is created endogenously to satisfy real credit demand through active commercial bank lending).[5][6]
🏦 Official Validation by Central Banks (BOE, etc.)
In 2014, the Bank of England formally stated in its Quarterly Bulletin (Money creation in the modern economy) that the textbook notion of banks acting merely as intermediaries lending out savers' deposits is incorrect; rather, banks actively create deposits via the act of lending. Keynes's insight has become the institutional consensus of contemporary central banking.[3][7][8]

🔍 4. Advanced Perspective: Evolution to Liquidity Preference in *The General Theory*

Following his formulation of actively created deposits in the 1930 Treatise on Money, Keynes expanded his framework in his 1936 magnum opus, The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money, introducing Liquidity Preference Theory to unpack the psychological motives behind holding liquid balances.[3][7][8]

Even though the commercial banking system can actively create deposits (purchasing power), if enterprises and investors retreat from capital formation due to fundamental uncertainty and hoard money instead, effective demand will stagnate. Keynesian monetary theory achieves its greatest depth by recognizing that the immense credit-creating capacity of banks and the dangers of speculative financial markets and liquidity hoarding are two interconnected sides of the same coin.
